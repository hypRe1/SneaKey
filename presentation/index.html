SOURCES: IEEE S&P 2004 · ACM CCS 2005 · IEEE EuroS&P Workshops 2023 (arXiv 2308.01074)

“Researchers have warned about this for 20 years, but every study made it easy for itself. Keys pressed one at a time, spelling correction doing the heavy lifting, or each key tapped 25 times in a row. Nobody types like that. We trained on people typing full sentences, which is what an attacker would actually hear.” If asked: past work recognised keys in isolation under controlled recording. Asonov’s typist pressed one key at a time; the 2023 study pressed each key 25 times in a row on a MacBook Pro, and its accuracy fell to roughly 40–64% with touch-typing. Zhuang’s raw result was about 60%; the 96% comes after English language constraints. How we differ: (1) continuous, natural typing, where keystrokes overlap, so finding each press is part of the problem; (2) the victim laptop’s own mic, with no phone, external mic or Zoom stream. That is the most realistic attack (any app with mic permission) and the hardest audio: centimetres from the keys, with chassis vibration and fan noise. Caveat: these accuracies use different tasks and setups, so they aren’t directly comparable with ours.

How SneaKey works

01

Find the clicks

Spot sudden jumps in loudness.

02

Keep real presses GBDT

Throw out key releases and background noise.

03

Name the key SVM

Match each press’s sound fingerprint to a key, with a score for every key.

04

Filter to real words LM

Keep only the likely letters, then pick the most English-looking text.

One sentence per step: 1. “First we find the clicks: moments where the sound suddenly jumps compared with the room’s background.” 2. “Not every click is a key press. Keys click on release too, so a gradient-boosted tree model checks 13 traits of each click and keeps the real presses.” 3. “For each press we take a 32 ms fingerprint of its pitches, and an SVM, which learned where each key’s fingerprints fall, scores every key.” Point at the figure: “Here it’s squashed to 2D. T’s clicks on one side, H’s on the other; a new click is named by which side it lands on.” 4. “That gives us a probability for each key, so only a few letters are possible per click. A language model then picks the combination that looks most like English.” Click any step box to show its diagram on the right (03 is shown by default). Clicks on the boxes don’t change slide. The figures are illustrations, not real data, apart from the sample-clip counts (257 bumps → 50 kept).
